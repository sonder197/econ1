Module 5.1 · Forex Quotes, Spreads and Triangular Arbitrage
LOS 5.a

Bid–Ask Spreads and Currency Quotes

Calculate and interpret the bid–offer spread on a spot or forward currency quotation and describe the factors that affect the bid–offer spread.

Exchange rates: base currency and price currency

An exchange rate is simply the price of one currency in terms of another. A quote of 1.4126 USD/EUR means each euro costs $1.4126. The euro is the base currency (bottom of the quote) and the USD is the price currency (top of the quote). A quote is therefore the price of one unit of the base currency in terms of the price currency.

Quote structure
A/B = units of A (price currency) per 1 unit of B (base currency)
A = price currency (numerator)
B = base currency (denominator)
Example = USD/EUR = 1.4126 → 1 EUR costs 1.4126 USD

Spot vs forward

⚡
Spot exchange rate

The rate for immediate delivery. For most currencies that means the exchange of currencies takes place two days after the trade.

📅
Forward exchange rate

The rate for an exchange to be done in the future. Forward rates are quoted for various dates (30 days, 60 days, 90 days, one year). A forward contract is an agreement to exchange a specific amount of one currency for a specific amount of another currency on a future date specified in the agreement.

Bid and offer (ask) quotes

Dealer quotes often include both a bid and an offer (ask) rate. Example: the euro quoted as $1.4124 − 1.4128. The bid ($1.4124) is the price at which the dealer will buy euros; the offer ($1.4128) is the price at which the dealer will sell euros. By convention quotes often show only the last two digits of the offer: $1.4124 − 28.

Professor's Note

The "bid–offer" spread is also known as the "bid–ask" spread: the terms "ask" and "offer" mean the same thing. They are used interchangeably.

The foreign exchange spread

The difference between the offer and the bid is the spread, often stated in pips. When the spot quote has four decimal places, one pip is 1/10,000. In the example above the spread is $0.0004 (4 pips), reflecting the dealer's profit. Dealers manage their foreign-currency inventories by trading in the interbank market (a wholesale market for currency), where spreads are narrow.

Bid–ask spread
spread = ask quote − bid quote
Example = 1.4128 − 1.4124 = 0.0004 = 4 pips

Slide The slides group currencies by how the spread is quoted in pips: (1) EUR, GBP, AUD, NZD, CAD, SGD, CHF, HKD, CNY, CNH; (2) JPY, THB; (3) VND. Study aid The grouping reflects the quoting convention: the first group is normally quoted to four decimals (pip = 0.0001) while JPY and THB are normally quoted to two decimals (pip = 0.01); the dong is a high-denomination currency quoted in large whole numbers. Always read the pip size off the number of decimals in the quote.

What drives the spread?

🏦
Dealer spread depends on…

  • The interbank spread for the same pair (dealer spreads vary directly with it)
  • The size of the transaction: larger, liquidity-demanding trades get a larger spread
  • The dealer–client relationship: preferred clients may get favorable rates

🌐
Interbank spread depends on…

  • Currencies involved: high-volume pairs (USD/EUR, USD/JPY, USD/GBP) have lower spreads than low-volume pairs (AUD/CAD)
  • Time of day: the New York–London overlap is the most liquid window, spreads are narrower
  • Volatility: higher volatility → higher spreads (compensates market makers for the risk of holding the currencies)

📆
Forward spreads increase with maturity

  • Longer-maturity contracts tend to be less liquid
  • Counterparty credit risk rises with maturity
  • Interest rate risk rises with maturity

Warm-up: working with FX quotes

A dealer sells a currency at the ask and buys it at the bid; the investor always takes the loss from the spread. Given a USD/AUD quote of 1.0508 − 1.0510: you can buy AUD (the base currency) at the ask of USD 1.0510 and sell AUD at the bid of USD 1.0508.

🅱️
Base currency (AUD)

Buy the base currency at the ask. Sell the base currency at the bid.

💲
Price currency (USD)

The opposite: buy the price currency at the bid (you are selling the base currency). Sell the price currency at the ask.

The shortcut that works every time: "up the bid and multiply, down the ask and divide"

Look at the quote as a fraction: price currency on top, base currency on the bottom. Converting from the bottom to the top (base → price currency) is going UP the quote: use the bid and multiply. Converting from the top to the bottom (price → base currency) is going DOWN the quote: use the ask and divide. For USD/AUD: USD → AUD is down (ask, divide); AUD → USD is up (bid, multiply).

Example — converting currencies using spot rates

A dealer quotes the AUD/GBP spot rate as 1.5060 − 1.5067 (GBP is the base). How do we (1) convert 1 million GBP and (2) convert 1 million AUD?

  1. GBP 1 million → AUD: from the base (bottom) to the price currency (top) = up the quote → bid 1.5060, multiply: 1,000,000 × 1.5060 = AUD 1,506,000.
  2. AUD 1 million → GBP: from the price currency (top) to the base (bottom) = down the quote → ask 1.5067, divide: 1,000,000 / 1.5067 = GBP 663,702 (rounded).
🧮 Quote converter — try the up/down rule
Key Concepts · LOS 5.a

Bid–ask spread (for the base currency) = ask quote − bid quote. Dealer spreads depend on the spreads in the interbank market, the transaction size and the dealer–client relationship. Interbank spreads depend on the currencies involved, the time of day and the volatility of the currency pair. Forward spreads increase with maturity (lower liquidity, rising counterparty credit risk and rising interest rate risk).

Cross Rates and Triangular Arbitrage

Identify a triangular arbitrage opportunity and calculate its profit, given the bid–offer quotations for three currencies.

Cross rates

The cross rate is the exchange rate between two currencies implied by their exchange rates with a common third currency. It is needed when there is no active FX market in the pair being considered. It must be computed from the rates between each of the two currencies and a major third currency, usually the USD or EUR.

Suppose USD/AUD = 0.60 and MXN/USD = 10.70. What is the cross rate MXN/AUD?

Make the common currency cancel
MXN/AUD = USD/AUD × MXN/USD = 0.60 × 10.70 = 6.42
Result = 6.42 pesos per Australian dollar
Key = arrange the quotes so that the common currency cancels (it appears once in a numerator and once in a denominator)

Cross rates with bid–ask spreads

Spreads complicate the calculation considerably. With three currencies A, B and C there are three pairs (A/B, A/C and B/C). To compute the cross rate A/C from A/B and B/C:

Rule 1 — chain the quotes (common currency B cancels)
(A/C)bid = (A/B)bid × (B/C)bid
(A/C)offer = (A/B)offer × (B/C)offer

If instead you are given A/B and C/B you must first obtain B/C, because A/B × C/B ≠ A/C. Invert the C/B quote — and note that the bid becomes the inverse of the offer, and vice versa:

Rule 2 — inverting a quote
(B/C)bid = 1 / (C/B)offer
(B/C)offer = 1 / (C/B)bid

Triangular arbitrage

Real-world dealers keep bid/ask quotes that guarantee a profit to the dealer no matter which currencies customers trade. If they did not, customers could earn profits by triangular arbitrage. Start with three currency pairs, each with bid and ask quotes, and build a triangle where each node is a currency. Go around the triangle clockwise (and then counter-clockwise) until you are back at the start currency, applying up-the-bid-multiply, down-the-ask-divide at each leg. If you end with more money than you started with, there is an arbitrage profit. The bid–ask spread forces you to buy a currency at a higher rate going one way than you can sell it for going the other way.

Example — triangular arbitrage

Interbank quotes: USD/AUD 0.6000 − 0.6015 and USD/MXN 0.0933 − 0.0935.

1. Implied MXN/AUD cross rate. We need (MXN/AUD)bid = (USD/AUD)bid × (MXN/USD)bid. Because we were given USD/MXN, invert first:

Invert USD/MXN to MXN/USD
(MXN/USD)bid = 1 / (USD/MXN)offer = 1/0.0935 = 10.70
(MXN/USD)offer = 1 / (USD/MXN)bid = 1/0.0933 = 10.72
Implied cross rate
(MXN/AUD)bid = 0.6000 × 10.70 = 6.42
(MXN/AUD)offer = 0.6015 × 10.72 = 6.4481
Implied = MXN/AUD = 6.42 − 6.4481

2. Is there arbitrage? Your dealer quotes MXN/AUD = 6.3000 − 6.3025, which falls outside the implied 6.42 − 6.4481, so profit may be possible (we must check). Use the dealer quotes in the triangle, not the computed cross rates. Start with USD 1 million.

Clockwise (USD → MXN → AUD → USD):

  1. USD → MXN: the quote is USD/MXN and we go down → ask 0.0935, divide: 1,000,000 / 0.0935 = MXN 10,695,187.
  2. MXN → AUD at the ask of 6.3025 (MXN/AUD, going down): 10,695,187 / 6.3025 = AUD 1,696,975.
  3. AUD → USD: the quote is USD/AUD and we go up → bid 0.6000, multiply: 1,696,975 × 0.60 = USD 1,018,185 → profit USD 18,185.

Counter-clockwise (USD → AUD → MXN → USD):

  1. USD → AUD at the ask of 0.6015, divide: 1,000,000 / 0.6015 = AUD 1,662,510.
  2. AUD → MXN at the bid of 6.3000 (MXN/AUD, going up), multiply: MXN 10,473,814 (the notes round; the unrounded figure is 10,473,815).
  3. MXN → USD at the bid of 0.0933, multiply: USD 977,207 → loss USD 22,793.
💡You can never earn an arbitrage profit in both directions. If one direction gains, the other loses. If the dealer's cross quote lies inside the implied range, both directions lose and there is no arbitrage.
Shortcut for choosing the direction Study aid

Compare the dealer's quote for the cross with the implied cross range. If the dealer's ask is below the implied bid (here 6.3025 < 6.42), the base currency (AUD) is cheap in the dealer market: buy it there with MXN and sell it through the USD route. If the dealer's bid is above the implied offer, sell the base currency at the dealer's bid and buy it back through the implied route. Always confirm by running the full triangle.

Key Concepts · LOS 5.b

To calculate the profit from triangular arbitrage, start in the home currency and go around the triangle: exchange the home currency for the first foreign currency, then the first for the second foreign currency, then the second back into the home currency. If you end up with more money than you started with, you earned an arbitrage profit. The bid–ask spread forces you to buy a currency at a higher rate going one way than you can sell it for going the other way.

Spot and Forward Rates; Forward Premium and Discount

Explain spot and forward rates and calculate the forward premium/discount for a given currency.

Spot and forward rates

The spot rate is for immediate delivery (usually two days after the trade); a forward rate is for an exchange at a future date (30, 60, 90 days, one year…), contracted today (see LOS 5.a).

Forward premium and discount

A currency is quoted at a forward premium relative to a second currency if the forward price (in units of the second currency) is greater than the spot price. It is at a forward discount if the forward price is less than the spot price. The premium or discount is for the base currency (the currency at the bottom of the quote). Example: spot = 1.20 $/€ and forward = 1.25 $/€ → the euro trades at a forward premium.

Forward premium (discount)
forward premium (discount) = F − S0
F = forward price (A/B)
S0 = spot price (A/B)
Rule = for an A/B quote, a positive result means currency B (the base currency) trades at a forward premium

Forward quotes in practice: points over spot

In FX markets forward quotes are usually presented as a premium or discount (in points/pips) over the spot rate. The all-in forward rate is the spot rate plus (minus) the forward premium (discount), converted at 1 pip = 1/10,000.

Example — spot and forward quotes

AUD/CAD quotes (CAD is the base). Compute the bid and offer for a 30-day forward contract.

MaturityRate (AUD/CAD)
Spot1.0511 / 1.0519
30-day+3.9 / +4.1
90-day+15.6 / +16.8
180-day+46.9 / +52.3

All forward points are positive, so the CAD (the base currency) trades at a forward premium.

All-in 30-day forward
30-day bid = 1.0511 + 3.9/10,000 = 1.05149
30-day offer = 1.0519 + 4.1/10,000 = 1.05231
Answer = the 30-day all-in forward quote for AUD/CAD is 1.05149 / 1.05231
Professor's Note

For an investor wishing to convert AUD into CAD in the forward market, the relevant quote is the ask rate (remember the "down-the-ask" rule) of 1.05231. This is the all-in rate: after adding (subtracting) the forward premium (discount) for the investor in question.

LOS 5.d

Mark-to-Market Value of a Forward Contract

Calculate the mark-to-market value of a forward contract.

Value of a forward contract over time

If the forward contract price is consistent with covered interest rate parity (LOS 5.e), the value of the contract at initiation is zero to both parties. After initiation, the value changes as the forward quotes for the currency pair change in the market.

Mark-to-market value

The value of a forward currency contract prior to expiration is its mark-to-market value. To compute it: take the difference between the forward price you locked in and the current forward price, multiply by the size of the contract, and discount for the time remaining until settlement.

Mark-to-market value of a forward contract
Vt = (FPt − FP)(contract size) / [1 + R(days/360)]
Vt = value of the forward contract at time t (to the party buying the base currency), t < T, denominated in the price currency
FPt = forward price (to sell the base currency) at time t, in the market for a new contract maturing at time T
FP = forward price specified in the contract at inception (to buy the base currency)
days = number of days remaining to maturity of the forward contract (T − t)
R = interest rate of the price currency
Professor's Note

The interest rate references used throughout this reading are the rates on bank deposits using the appropriate market reference rate (MRR), based on the days/360 convention. MRR is the generic label used in the CFA curriculum to represent the various LIBOR successors globally.

Why the bid, and why the price-currency rate?

The MTM value is what you would receive if you unwound the position today by entering an offsetting forward with the same maturity. If you are long the base currency, unwinding means selling the base currency forward — going up the quote, so you use the bid for the new contract. The gain or loss is a price-currency amount received at maturity, so it is discounted with the price-currency interest rate for the remaining days.

Example — valuing a forward contract prior to maturity

Yew Mun Yip entered a 90-day forward, long CAD 1 million against AUD, at 1.05358 AUD/CAD. Thirty days later, the AUD/CAD quotes are:

MaturityFX rate (AUD/CAD)
Spot1.0612 / 1.0614
30-day+4.9 / +5.2
60-day+8.6 / +9.0
90-day+14.6 / +16.8
180-day+42.3 / +48.3
AUD interest rates (at t = 30)Rate
30-day1.12%
60-day1.16%
90-day1.20%

Yip is long CAD (converted AUD into CAD). To unwind, he sells CAD for AUD: up the bid. After 30 days, 60 days remain. The new 60-day forward bid is 1.0612 + 8.6/10,000 = 1.06206. Discount with the 60-day AUD rate, 1.16%:

Mark-to-market (Yip)
Vt = (1.06206 − 1.05358)(1,000,000) / [1 + 0.0116(60/360)] = 8,480 / 1.001933 = AUD 8,463.64

Thirty days in, Yip's position has gained AUD 8,463.64 because the CAD (the currency he is long) has appreciated against the AUD since inception. He could close out the contract that day and receive AUD 8,463.64. Be sure to use the AUD (price currency) interest rate.

Extension: if you are SHORT the base currency Study aid

The formula in the notes is the value to the party buying the base currency. A short-base party unwinds by buying the base back at the new forward offer, so its value is (FP − FPt,offer)(size) / [1 + R(days/360)]. The logic is the same: offset with a same-maturity forward, take the dealer's side of the spread against you, and discount at the price-currency rate.

Key Concepts · LOS 5.d

The mark-to-market value of a forward contract reflects the profit that would be realized by closing out the position at current market prices — equivalent to offsetting the contract with an equal and opposite forward position: Vt = (FPt − FP)(contract size) / [1 + R(days/360)], where days is the number of days remaining and R is the price-currency interest rate.

International Parity Conditions

Explain international parity conditions: covered and uncovered interest rate parity, forward rate parity, purchasing power parity, and the international Fisher effect.

Covered interest rate parity (CIP)

"Covered" means bound by arbitrage. Covered interest rate parity holds when any forward premium or discount exactly offsets the difference in interest rates, so an investor earns the same return investing in either currency. If euro interest rates are higher than dollar rates, the forward discount on the euro relative to the dollar will just offset the higher euro interest rate. For an A/B quote:

Covered interest rate parity
F = S0 × [1 + RA(days/360)] / [1 + RB(days/360)]
F = forward rate (quoted as A/B)
S0 = spot rate (quoted as A/B)
days = number of days in the underlying forward contract
RA, RB = interest rates for currency A and currency B
Professor's Note

For all parity relations, follow the numerator–denominator rule: if you are given a USD/EUR quote, the USD interest rate goes in the numerator and the EUR interest rate in the denominator of the parity equation.

Recall that the forward premium (discount) can therefore be written as:

Forward premium (discount) under CIP
F − S0 = S0 {[1 + RA(days/360)] / [1 + RB(days/360)] − 1}
or
F − S0 = S0 × [(days/360) / (1 + RB(days/360))] × (RA − RB)

Example — covered interest arbitrage

USD MRR = 8%, EUR MRR = 6%. Spot = $1.30 per euro (USD/EUR); the 1-year forward is $1.35 per euro. Is there a profitable arbitrage?

Parity would require F = $1.30 × (1.08 / 1.06) = $1.3245. The market forward ($1.35) is higher than the parity value, so the forward euro is too expensive: sell euros forward and buy euros spot. Per $1,000:

Initially:

  1. Borrow $1,000 at 8%.
  2. Buy 1,000 / 1.30 = 769.23 euros in the spot market.
  3. Invest the euros at 6%.
  4. Enter a forward contract to sell the expected proceeds in one year (769.23 × 1.06 = 815.38 euros) at $1.35 each.

After one year:

  1. Sell the 815.38 euros under the forward at $1.35 → $1,100.76.
  2. Repay the $1,000 loan: $1,080.
  3. Keep the difference of $20.76 as arbitrage profit.

Uncovered interest rate parity (UIP)

CIP uses arbitrage to force the forward rate to a level consistent with the interest-rate differential. If forward contracts are not available, or if capital flows are restricted so as to prevent arbitrage, the relationship need not hold. Uncovered interest rate parity refers to such a situation: "uncovered" means not bound by arbitrage.

Country A has a 4% rate and Country B 9%. Under UIP, currency B is expected to depreciate 5% a year relative to currency A, so an investor is indifferent between investing in A or B. For an A/B quote, the base currency (B) is expected to appreciate by approximately RA − RB (when RA − RB is negative, B is expected to depreciate):

Uncovered interest rate parity
E(%ΔSA/B) = RA − RB
Reading = the higher-interest-rate currency is expected to depreciate by roughly the interest differential
Use = a forecast of the future spot rate (not a market-traded rate)

Example — forecasting spot rates with UIP

Spot ZAR/EUR = 8.385. One-year nominal rates: eurozone 10%, South Africa 8%. The rand rate is lower than the euro rate, so UIP predicts the rand will rise in value (fewer rand per euro). The euro (base) is expected to "appreciate" by approximately RZAR − REUR = 8% − 10% = −2% — i.e., the euro depreciates 2% against the rand: 8.385 × 0.98 = 8.217 ZAR/EUR over the coming year.

Covered vs uncovered

Covered interest parity derives the no-arbitrage forward rate; uncovered interest parity derives the expected future spot rate (which is not market-traded). Covered parity is enforced by arbitrage; uncovered parity is not.

UIP assumes a risk-neutral investor; forward rate parity

Under UIP, if the foreign interest rate is 2% higher, the foreign currency is expected to depreciate 2%, so the investor is indifferent between foreign and domestic investment. An investor who invests abroad without any extra return (the rate differential is offset by the currency move) is not demanding a risk premium for foreign-currency risk. Hence UIP assumes the investor is risk-neutral.

If the forward rate equals the expected future spot rate, the forward rate is an unbiased predictor of the future spot rate: F = E(ST), called forward rate parity. In this special case, if CIP holds (and it will, by arbitrage), UIP also holds — and vice versa. Stated differently: if UIP holds, forward rate parity also holds.

Forward rate parity
F = E(ST)
Meaning = the forward rate is an unbiased predictor of the future spot rate
💡There is no reason UIP must hold in the short run, and it typically does not. There is evidence that it holds in the long run, so longer-term expected spot rates from UIP are often used as forecasts of future exchange rates.

The Fisher relation and the international Fisher effect

Irving Fisher: the nominal rate of return is (approximately) the real rate plus expected inflation — the (domestic) Fisher relation.

Fisher relation
Rnominal = Rreal + E(inflation)

Under real interest rate parity, real interest rates are assumed to converge across markets: with free capital flows, funds move to the country with the higher real rate until real rates are equalized. Combining the Fisher relation with real rate parity gives the international Fisher effect:

International Fisher effect
Rnominal A − Rnominal B = E(inflationA) − E(inflationB)
Meaning = the difference between two countries' nominal interest rates equals the difference between their expected inflation rates

Examples — Fisher

Real rate. South African nominal rate 9.0%, expected inflation 3.5%: real rate = 0.090 − 0.035 = 0.055 = 5.5%.

International Fisher. Eurozone expected inflation 9.0%, South African expected inflation 13.0%, eurozone nominal rate 10.09%. Real rate (EUR = ZAR) ≈ 10.09% − 9% = 1.09%. Nominal ZAR rate = 13% + 1.09% = 14.09%.

Purchasing power parity (PPP)

The law of one price: identical goods should have the same price in all locations (after adjusting for the exchange rate). If designer jeans are cheaper in New York than in Paris, arbitrageurs buy in New York and sell in Paris until the gap closes. In practice the law of one price does not hold because of frictions such as tariffs and transportation costs.

Absolute PPP compares the average price of a representative basket of consumption goods between countries using a price index such as the CPI. It requires only that the law of one price be right on average.

Absolute PPP
SA/B = CPIA / CPIB

Even if the law of one price held for every good, absolute PPP might fail because the weights (consumption patterns) of the goods in the two economies differ (people eat more potatoes in Russia and more rice in Japan).

Relative PPP states that changes in exchange rates should exactly offset the price effects of any inflation differential between two countries. If Country A has 6% inflation and Country B 4% over a year, A's currency should depreciate by about 2% against B's.

Relative PPP
%ΔSA/B = inflationA − inflationB
%ΔSA/B = change in the spot price (A/B)

Relative PPP rests on the idea that even if absolute PPP fails, there may still be a relationship between exchange-rate changes and inflation differences. The ex-ante version of PPP is the same as relative PPP except that it uses expected inflation instead of actual inflation.

Example — ex-ante PPP

Spot USD/AUD = 1.00. Expected annual inflation: Australia 5%, United States 2%. AUD has the higher expected inflation, so it should depreciate against the USD. Expected change = inflationUSD − inflationAUD = 2% − 5% = −3% → new USD/AUD ≈ 0.97.

💡Because there is no true arbitrage to force relative PPP to hold, short-run violations are common. Evidence suggests it holds approximately in the long run, so it remains a useful method for estimating the relationship between exchange rates and inflation rates.

Study aid Which sign? One rule for every parity formula

In an A/B quote, a positive %ΔS means the base currency B appreciated (and A depreciated). Both UIP and relative PPP say: the currency with the higher interest rate (UIP) or the higher inflation (PPP) is the one expected to depreciate — that currency is in the numerator (A) when RA − RB > 0, so the quote rises.

Practical implications of the full framework (from the Module Quiz 5.2 answer key)

Combining all parity relationships, the expected return on risk-free securities should be the same in all countries and exchange rate risk is really just inflation risk. Four implications:

  1. The real, risk-free return will be the same in all countries.
  2. Investing in countries with high nominal interest rates will not generate excess returns, because the high nominal rates are accompanied by local-currency depreciation.
  3. All investors will earn the same expected return in their own currency on any investment denominated in a foreign currency.
  4. Exchange rate risk is simply inflation risk, so investors interested in real returns will not face exchange rate risk.

Hence the claim that "there are significant rewards for bearing foreign exchange risk" is not an implication of the framework.

The international Fisher effect links interest-rate and inflation differentials. Relative PPP links inflation differentials to exchange-rate changes. Uncovered interest parity links interest differentials to expected exchange-rate changes.

Forecasting Spot Rates and Long-Run Fair Value

5.g: Evaluate the use of the current spot rate, the forward rate, purchasing power parity, and uncovered interest parity to forecast future spot exchange rates. 5.h: Explain approaches to assessing the long-run fair value of an exchange rate.

5.g — Forecasting future spot rates

We can use ex-ante PPP, uncovered interest rate parity or forward rates to forecast future spot rates. UIP and PPP are not bound by arbitrage and seldom work over the short and medium terms. Similarly, the forward rate is not an unbiased predictor of the future spot rate in practice. However, PPP holds over reasonably long horizons.

5.h — Long-run fair value of an exchange rate

If relative PPP held at every point in time, the real exchange rate (the exchange rate adjusted for relative historical inflation between the currency pair) would be constant. Since relative PPP seldom holds over the short term, the real exchange rate fluctuates around its mean-reverting equilibrium value — that equilibrium is the long-run fair-value anchor.

Real exchange rate (illustrative path)Equilibrium real rate — what relative PPP implies (constant)
📈
Currency above its equilibrium

A currency whose real value has risen above its historical mean is overvalued per PPP and a candidate to depreciate toward fair value (this is also one of the currency-crisis warning signs, LOS 5.m).

⚖️
Caveat — sovereign risk premia

The international Fisher effect (and real rate parity) assumes no differences in sovereign risk premia (all countries perceived as equally risky). That is untrue: investors demand a higher real return (a risk premium) for emerging-market currencies perceived as riskier.

Key Concepts · LOS 5.g, 5.h

Future spot rates can be forecast using PPP or uncovered interest rate parity, but neither is bound by arbitrage and neither necessarily works in the short term. Forward rates can be estimated with covered interest parity, which is bound by arbitrage. If uncovered interest parity holds, forward rate parity holds, i.e., the forward rate is an unbiased estimate of the future spot rate.

The Carry Trade and Uncovered Interest Rate Parity

Describe the carry trade and its relation to uncovered interest rate parity and calculate the profit from a carry trade.

The FX carry trade

Uncovered interest rate parity says a currency with a high interest rate should depreciate against a currency with a lower rate, so an investor earns the same return in either. If short-term rates are 3% in the U.K. and 1% in the U.S., UIP implies GBP should depreciate 2% against USD over the year.

But UIP is not bound by arbitrage. If the GBP depreciates by less than 2% (or even appreciates), an investor who invested in the higher-yielding GBP using funds borrowed in USD earns excess profit. In an FX carry trade the investor invests in a higher-yielding currency using funds borrowed in a lower-yielding currency. The lower-yielding currency is the funding currency.

Carry trade profit
return = interest earned on investment − funding cost − currency depreciation
Equivalent = profit on carry trade = interest differential − change in the spot rate of the investment currency
Sign = depreciation of the investment currency reduces profit; appreciation adds to it

Example — carry trade

Interest rates: U.K. 3%, U.S. 1%. USD/GBP spot today 1.50; one year later 1.50. Profit to an investor borrowing in the U.S. and investing in the U.K.:

Profit
return = 3% − 1% − 0% = 2%
Why 0% = the exchange rate did not change (1.50 → 1.50), so there is no currency depreciation
🎯
What the trade is

A bet against uncovered interest rate parity. It tries to capture the interest-rate differential.

🌤️
When it works

Carry trades typically perform well in low-volatility periods. Sometimes higher yields attract larger capital inflows → economic boom and appreciation (instead of depreciation) of the high-yielding currency — which makes the trade even more profitable (interest spread + currency gain).

Risks of the carry trade

The carry trade is profitable only if UIP does not hold over the investment horizon. The risk is that the funding currency may appreciate significantly against the investment currency, reducing profit or causing a loss. Furthermore, the return distribution is not normal: it has negative skewness and excess kurtosis (fat tails), so the probability of a large loss is higher than a normal distribution implies. This high probability of a large loss is the crash risk of the carry trade.

Carry trade (negative skew, excess kurtosis)Normal

Where crash risk comes from. It stems from the carry trade's leveraged nature (borrow the funding currency, invest in the high-yielding one). As more investors follow, demand for the high-yielding currency pushes its value up. But with this herding behavior comes the risk that all investors try to exit at the same time (especially if they use stop-loss orders). In turbulent times, as investors exit — a flight to safety — the high-yielding currency can fall steeply, producing large losses.

💡Exam trap: carry-trade returns have negative skewness and excess kurtosis. Saying "negative kurtosis and excess skewness" (as Poulsen does in the Module Quiz) is wrong on both terms.

Key Concepts · LOS 5.i

The FX carry trade seeks to profit from the failure of UIP to work in the short run: the investor invests in a high-yielding currency while borrowing in a low-yielding one. If the higher-yielding currency does not depreciate by the interest-rate differential, the investor makes a profit. Profit on carry trade = interest differential − change in the spot rate of the investment currency. The carry trade has exposure to crash risk.

Balance of Payments and Exchange Rates

Explain how flows in the balance of payment accounts affect currency exchange rates.

Balance of payments (BOP) accounting

Balance-of-payments accounting keeps track of transactions between a country and its international trading partners. It includes government, consumer and business transactions, and reflects all payments and liabilities to foreigners as well as all payments and obligations received from foreigners.

🛒
Current account

Measures the exchange of goods, services, investment income and unilateral transfers (gifts to and from other nations). Surplus = selling more goods and services to the rest of the world than buying from it. Deficit = buying more than selling.

💸
Financial account (a.k.a. capital account)

Measures the flow of funds for debt and equity investment into and out of the country.

💡When a country runs a current account deficit, it must generate a surplus in its capital account (or see its currency depreciate). Capital flows are the dominant influence on exchange rates in the short term because they are larger and change more rapidly than goods flows.

Current account influences on exchange rates

Current account deficits lead to a depreciation of the domestic currency through a variety of mechanisms:

🔄
1 · Flow supply/demand mechanism

Deficits increase the supply of the currency in the markets (exporters to that country convert their revenues into their own currency) → downward pressure on its value. The depreciation may restore balance, depending on: (i) the size of the initial deficit (bigger deficit → bigger depreciation needed); (ii) the influence of the exchange rate on domestic import and export prices (not all of the higher import cost may be passed on to consumers); (iii) price elasticity of demand of the traded goods (if key imports are price inelastic, the quantity imported will not change).

🧺
2 · Portfolio balance mechanism

Surplus countries usually have capital account deficits, typically investments in the deficit countries. Investor countries may find their portfolios dominated by a few investee currencies. When they decide to rebalance, it can have a significant negative impact on those investee-country currencies.

🏚️
3 · Debt sustainability mechanism

A deficit country may run a capital account surplus by borrowing from abroad. When debt gets too high relative to GDP, investors may question sustainability → rapid depreciation of the borrower's currency.

Capital account influences on exchange rates

Capital account flows are one of the major determinants of exchange rates. As capital flows into a country, demand for its currency rises → appreciation. Differences in real rates of return are a major determinant of capital flows: higher relative real returns attract foreign capital. Capital inflows may be needed to overcome a shortage of domestic savings to fund the investment required for growth. But inflows in excess of needed investment capital cause problems, especially for emerging markets:

  • Excessive real appreciation of the domestic currency.
  • Financial asset and/or real estate bubbles.
  • Increases in external debt by businesses or government.
  • Excessive consumption in the domestic market fueled by credit.

Emerging-market governments often counteract excessive inflows by imposing capital controls or by direct intervention in the FX market (see LOS 5.l).

LOS 5.k

Effects of Monetary and Fiscal Policy on Exchange Rates

Explain the potential effects of monetary and fiscal policy on exchange rates.

Mundell-Fleming model

Developed in the early 1960s, the Mundell-Fleming model evaluates the short-term impact of monetary and fiscal policy on interest rates — and consequently on exchange rates. It assumes there is sufficient slack in the economy to handle changes in aggregate demand, so inflation is not a concern: changes in inflation caused by policy are not explicitly modeled.

Flexible exchange rate regimes

In a flexible (floating) system, rates are set by supply and demand in the FX market. The influence of policy depends on capital mobility.

🌊
High capital mobility (developed markets)

Expansionary monetary policy ↓ real interest rates → ↓ capital inflows (financial account deteriorates) → ↓ demand for the currency → depreciation. Restrictive monetary policy: opposite (appreciation).

Expansionary fiscal policy (bigger deficit from lower taxes or higher spending) ↑ government borrowing → ↑ real rates → attracts foreign investment → improves financial account → ↑ demand for the currency → appreciation.

→ Monetary and fiscal policy have opposite effects.

🚧
Low capital mobility (emerging markets)

Capital flows may be restricted, so the goods-flow (trade imbalance) effect is greater than the interest-rate (financial-flow) effect. Expansionary fiscal or monetary policy → ↑ net imports → depreciation. Restrictive monetary or fiscal policy → appreciation.

→ Monetary and fiscal policy push in the same direction.

Professor's Note

Candidates are often confused by the Mundell-Fleming implication that a higher-interest-rate currency will appreciate relative to a lower-interest-rate currency, because this is exactly the opposite of what we learned under uncovered interest rate parity. Note that UIP assumed real interest rates are equal globally, so nominal rates merely mirror expected inflation. That condition no longer holds under Mundell-Fleming, which does not consider inflation.

Fixed exchange rate regimes

Under a fixed regime the government fixes the exchange rate of its currency against a major currency. An expansionary (restrictive) monetary policy would lead to depreciation (appreciation) of the domestic currency; to defend the peg the government must then purchase (sell) its own currency in the FX market, which essentially reverses the expansionary (restrictive) stance. So in a world with capital mobility, governments cannot both manage exchange rates and pursue independent monetary policy. To manage monetary policy they must either let the currency float freely or restrict capital movements. Slide The slides name this the impossible trinity.

Monetary approach to exchange rate determination

Monetary models only take into account the effect of monetary policy (fiscal effects are not considered). Unlike Mundell-Fleming, they assume output is fixed so that monetary policy primarily affects inflation, which in turn affects exchange rates. Two main approaches:

1. Pure monetary model. PPP holds at any point in time and output is constant. Expansionary (restrictive) monetary policy → prices rise (fall) → the domestic currency loses (gains) value. An x% increase in the money supply → x% increase in price levels → x% depreciation of the domestic currency. It does not take into account expectations about future monetary expansion or contraction.

2. Dornbusch overshooting model. Prices are sticky (inflexible) in the short term and do not immediately reflect monetary policy (PPP does not hold in the short term). Exchange rates therefore overshoot the long-run PPP value in the short term. Expansionary policy lowers interest rates and causes a larger-than-PPP-implied depreciation (due to capital outflows); over time the exchange rate gradually moves back toward the PPP value. Restrictive policy → excessive appreciation in the short term, then slow depreciation toward the long-term PPP value.

Portfolio balance approach to exchange rate determination

The portfolio balance approach focuses only on the effects of fiscal policy (not monetary). While Mundell-Fleming looks at the short-term effects of fiscal policy, this approach takes a long-term view of a sustained fiscal deficit or surplus. A government running a deficit borrows from investors, who evaluate the debt on expected risk and return. A sovereign-debt investor earns a return from both the debt's yield and the currency's return (when we hold a foreign-currency security, our realized return = local return + the foreign currency's performance against our own). When a government keeps up a long-term expansionary fiscal stance, investors assess the implications for risk and return (typically the yield should rise because of a higher risk premium). If investors perceive the yield and/or currency return as sufficient, they keep buying the bonds. But continued deficit increases are unsustainable and investors may refuse to fund the deficits → currency depreciation.

Combining Mundell-Fleming and portfolio balance

Short term, with free capital flows: expansionary fiscal policy → domestic currency appreciation (via high interest rates). Long term: the government must reverse course (tighter fiscal policy) → depreciation. If it does not reverse, it will have to monetize its debt (print money — monetary expansion), which also leads to depreciation.

Central Bank Intervention and Capital Controls

Describe objectives of central bank or government intervention and capital controls and describe the effectiveness of intervention and capital controls.

Why capital flows need managing

A combination of "push" and "pull" factors determines capital flows into a country.

🧲
Pull factors

Favorable developments that make a country an attractive destination for foreign capital: relative price stability, a flexible exchange rate regime, an improved fiscal position, privatization of state-owned enterprises, etc.

🚀
Push factors

Largely driven by mobile international capital seeking high returns from a diversified portfolio.

Capital flows can lead to excessive appreciation of the currency, causing problems such as loss of export competitiveness in global markets, asset price bubbles and excessive consumption fueled by credit creation. Large inflows can also lead to a currency crisis when the capital is eventually withdrawn. To reduce these problems, policymakers may impose capital controls or have the central bank intervene directly in the FX market.

Objectives of capital controls and central bank intervention

1️⃣
Prevent excessive appreciation

Ensure the domestic currency does not appreciate excessively.

2️⃣
Protect independent monetary policy

Allow independent monetary policy without being hindered by its impact on currency values. Example: an EM central bank fighting inflation raises rates, but higher rates attract large inflows that push the currency up.

3️⃣
Reduce capital inflow

Reduce the aggregate volume of inflow of foreign capital.

Effectiveness

🏛️
Developed markets: relatively ineffective

Trading volume in the currency is usually very large relative to the central bank's FX reserves. Evidence shows central banks are relatively ineffective in intervening because of insufficient resources.

🌱
Emerging markets: evidence less clear

Central banks may accumulate sufficient FX reserves (relative to trading volume) to affect supply and demand of their currencies in the FX market.

💡The success of intervention depends on the size of official FX reserves relative to the average trading volume in the currency.
Link to the Module Quiz (Zambola)

To reduce the inflow of foreign capital, a government should pursue policies that make yields less attractive — e.g., expansionary monetary policy lowers interest rates — not raise rates (which attracts inflows and appreciates the currency). Raising rates to reduce inflation, or policies consistent with appreciation, would draw capital in.

Key Concepts · LOS 5.l

Capital controls and central bank intervention aim to reduce excessive capital inflows, which could lead to speculative bubbles. The success of central bank intervention depends on the size of official FX reserves at the central bank's disposal relative to the average trading volume in the currency. For developed markets, central bank resources are too small on a relative basis to be effective at managing exchange rates. However, some emerging-market countries with large FX reserves relative to trading volume have been somewhat effective.

Warning Signs of a Currency Crisis

Describe warning signs of a currency crisis.

Warning signs of a currency crisis

History shows market participants have failed to predict crises and are typically surprised by them. When market sentiment shifts significantly, crises may occur even for countries with sound economic fundamentals. The following conditions have been identified as warning signs in the period leading up to a currency crisis:

  1. Terms of trade (ratio of exports to imports) deteriorate.
  2. Fixed or partially fixed exchange rates (versus floating exchange rates).
  3. Official foreign exchange reserves dramatically decline.
  4. Currency value has risen above its historical mean.
  5. Inflation increases.
  6. Liberalized capital markets that allow free flow of capital.
  7. Money supply relative to bank reserves increases.
  8. Banking crises (may also be coincident).
